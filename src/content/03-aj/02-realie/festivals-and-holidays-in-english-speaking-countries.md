## Public holidays

In Britain, public holidays are called **bank holidays**, because banks and many offices close. There are only eight of them in England and Wales, and many fall on a **Monday**, so people get a **long weekend**. In the USA there are eleven **federal holidays**; many of them are also on Mondays.

## Holidays through the year

| Date | Holiday | Where | How it is celebrated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 31 Dec / 1 Jan | **New Year's Eve / New Year's Day** | everywhere | parties, fireworks, singing *Auld Lang Syne* at midnight, making **New Year's resolutions**; in Scotland it is called **Hogmanay** |
| 3rd Monday in January | **Martin Luther King Jr. Day** | USA | remembers the leader of the civil rights movement |
| 14 February | **St Valentine's Day** | everywhere | cards (often anonymous), flowers and chocolates for the person you love |
| 3rd Monday in February | **Presidents' Day** | USA | honours George Washington and other presidents |
| 17 March | **St Patrick's Day** | Ireland, but also the USA, UK, Canada… | the patron saint of Ireland; people wear green, parades (the biggest in New York and Dublin), Irish music, beer |
| 1 April | **April Fools' Day** | everywhere | practical jokes until noon; even newspapers and TV publish fake news |
| March / April | **Easter** | everywhere | Good Friday, Easter Sunday and Easter Monday; **chocolate eggs**, **Easter egg hunts** for children, **hot cross buns**; the Easter Bunny brings sweets |
| 2nd Sunday in May (USA) / March (UK) | **Mother's Day** | everywhere | cards, flowers and breakfast in bed for mums |
| last Monday in May | **Memorial Day** | USA | remembers soldiers who died in wars; the unofficial start of summer |
| 1 July | **Canada Day** | Canada | the founding of Canada in 1867 |
| 4 July | **Independence Day** | USA | the Declaration of Independence (1776); parades, barbecues, picnics and **fireworks** – the most important American holiday |
| 31 October | **Halloween** | mainly USA, UK, Ireland, Canada | children dress up as ghosts, witches or monsters and go **trick-or-treating**; people carve **pumpkins** into **jack-o'-lanterns** |
| 5 November | **Guy Fawkes Night / Bonfire Night** | UK | remembers the failed **Gunpowder Plot** of 1605 to blow up Parliament; **bonfires** and fireworks |
| 11 November | **Remembrance Day / Veterans Day** | UK, Commonwealth / USA | the end of the First World War (1918); two minutes' silence at 11 a.m., people wear red **poppies** |
| 4th Thursday in November | **Thanksgiving** | USA (Canada in October) | remembers the harvest feast of the Pilgrims in 1621; a big family dinner; the next day is **Black Friday**, the start of Christmas shopping |
| 30 November | **St Andrew's Day** | Scotland | the patron saint of Scotland |
| 25 December | **Christmas Day** | everywhere | see below |
| 26 December | **Boxing Day** | UK, Commonwealth | originally a day when servants received gifts in boxes; today sport and big sales |

## Christmas in Britain and the USA

Preparations start in November with lights, decorations and Christmas shopping. People send **Christmas cards** and decorate their homes with a Christmas tree, holly and **mistletoe** – couples are supposed to kiss under it. On **Christmas Eve** children hang up **stockings** at the end of their beds or by the fireplace and leave a mince pie and a glass of milk or sherry for **Father Christmas** (**Santa Claus**), who comes down the chimney at night. Presents are opened on the morning of **25 December**. In Britain the King's **Christmas message** is broadcast on TV in the afternoon, and people pull **Christmas crackers** – paper tubes which bang when you pull them and contain a paper crown, a small toy and a joke.

## Other cultural events

- **The Edinburgh Festival** and **Fringe** in August – theatre, comedy and music all over the city.
- **The Notting Hill Carnival** in London – a huge Caribbean street festival in August.
- **Wimbledon**, the **Boat Race** and the **Last Night of the Proms** in Britain; the **Super Bowl**, **Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade** and **Mardi Gras** in New Orleans in the USA.
- **Burns Night** (25 January) – Scots celebrate their national poet Robert Burns with haggis and whisky.

## Festive food compared with the Czech Republic

| Occasion | UK / USA | Czech Republic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Christmas | **roast turkey** with stuffing, roast potatoes, Brussels sprouts and cranberry sauce; **Christmas pudding** with brandy butter and mince pies (UK) | fish soup, **fried carp with potato salad** on Christmas Eve, Christmas biscuits, vánočka |
| main day | 25 December – lunch or dinner | 24 December – dinner |
| Easter | chocolate eggs, hot cross buns, roast lamb | lamb-shaped cake (beránek), mazanec, painted eggs, nettle soup |
| Thanksgiving | turkey, mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes, **pumpkin pie** | – |
| Halloween | sweets, toffee apples, pumpkin soup | – |
| New Year | parties, champagne | lentil soup for money, pork (the pig brings luck), no poultry ("luck would fly away") |

The biggest difference is that Christmas dinner in the Czech Republic is on Christmas Eve and is based on fish, while in English-speaking countries the main meal is on Christmas Day and is based on roast turkey.

## Useful phrases

- *It is celebrated on… / It falls on…*
- *It commemorates… / It dates back to…*
- *It is customary to… / People traditionally…*
- *Unlike Czechs, the British open their presents on…*
- *The tradition I like most is…, because…*
- *I think Halloween has become popular here because…*
