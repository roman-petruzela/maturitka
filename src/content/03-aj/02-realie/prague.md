The history is cut down to the milestones you can be asked about, and the sights are sorted by the historic districts, so you can describe a walk through the city. ## Basic facts

- **Capital** of the Czech Republic and its largest city, with about **1.4 million** inhabitants.
- Situated in the centre of **Bohemia**, on both banks of the **Vltava**, which is crossed by more than a dozen bridges.
- The **political centre** of the country – the seat of the President, the government and Parliament – and also its economic and cultural heart.
- Its historic centre has been a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** since 1992.
- Nicknames: **the City of a Hundred Spires** (in the early 19th century the mathematician Bernard Bolzano counted 103 of them) and **the Golden City**.

## History in milestones

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| legend | Princess **Libuše** looked down from Vyšehrad and prophesied a great city "whose glory would touch the stars". |
| 9th century | The **Přemyslid** princes founded **Prague Castle** (around 880); Vyšehrad later became their second seat. |
| 14th century | **Charles IV**, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor, made Prague the capital of the Empire. He founded **Charles University** (1348, the oldest university in Central Europe), the **New Town** and **Charles Bridge**, and started building **St Vitus Cathedral**. |
| 15th century | Prague became the centre of the **Hussite movement** inspired by the religious reformer Jan Hus; in 1420 Jan Žižka defeated a crusade on Vítkov Hill. |
| around 1600 | Emperor **Rudolf II** moved his court to Prague and invited artists and scientists, such as the astronomers Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler. |
| 1618–1621 | The **uprising of the Czech Estates** started the Thirty Years' War; after the defeat at **White Mountain** (1620), 27 leaders of the uprising were executed on Old Town Square (1621). |
| 19th century | The **National Revival** – the **National Theatre** was built with money collected by ordinary Czechs ("the nation for itself"). |
| 1918 | Prague became the capital of independent **Czechoslovakia**. |
| 1939–1945 | Nazi occupation, ended by the **Prague Uprising** in May 1945. |
| 1968 | The Soviet-led invasion crushed the reforms of the **Prague Spring**; "normalisation" followed. |
| 1989 | The **Velvet Revolution** started with a student demonstration on 17 November and brought down communism. |
| 1993 | After the peaceful **split of Czechoslovakia**, Prague became the capital of the Czech Republic. |

## The historic districts and their sights

The historic centre consists of five old towns: **Hradčany** (the Castle District) and **the Lesser Town** on the left bank, and **the Old Town**, **Josefov** (the former Jewish Town) and **the New Town** on the right bank.

### Hradčany – the Castle District

**Prague Castle** dominates the city. Once the seat of Czech kings, it is now the official residence of the President, and it is considered the largest ancient castle complex in the world.

- **St Vitus Cathedral** – the largest church in the country and a masterpiece of Gothic architecture. Its construction began in 1344 under the architects Matthias of Arras and Peter Parler, and it took almost six hundred years to complete (1929). Czech kings were crowned and buried here, and the **Czech Crown Jewels** are kept in a chamber above **St Wenceslas Chapel**, whose walls are decorated with semi-precious stones.
- **The Old Royal Palace** with **Vladislav Hall**, where official ceremonies, such as the inauguration of the President, take place.
- **St George's Basilica** – the best-preserved Romanesque church in Prague.
- **Golden Lane** – a row of tiny colourful houses built into the castle walls, where castle guards and craftsmen used to live.

### The Lesser Town

A picturesque quarter of narrow streets, Baroque palaces and gardens below the Castle. Many embassies have their seat here.

- **St Nicholas Church** – one of the finest examples of Czech Baroque architecture.
- **Nerudova Street** – a steep street leading up to the Castle, known for its **house signs**, which were used instead of house numbers. The writer Jan Neruda lived in the house At the Two Suns.
- **Kampa Island**, separated from the Lesser Town by a narrow stream called Čertovka, with old mill wheels.

### Charles Bridge

The most famous bridge in Prague connects the Lesser Town with the Old Town. Its construction began in 1357, and legend has it that the builders added eggs to the mortar to make it stronger. It is 516 metres long, guarded by Gothic towers at both ends and lined with **thirty Baroque statues** of saints. The best known is the statue of **St John of Nepomuk**, who, according to legend, was thrown into the Vltava because he refused to reveal the Queen's confession to the King. People believe that if you touch the relief on its base, you will come back to Prague. Today the bridge is for pedestrians only and is full of tourists, artists and street musicians.

### The Old Town and Josefov

- **Old Town Square** used to be the main marketplace and was the scene of many historical events. It is dominated by the Gothic **Týn Church**, and in the middle stands the **Jan Hus Memorial**. Twenty-seven crosses in the pavement mark the place where the leaders of the uprising were executed in 1621.
- **The Old Town Hall** has the famous **Astronomical Clock** (1410), the oldest astronomical clock in the world that still works. Every hour the twelve Apostles appear in its windows.
- **The Estates Theatre**, where Mozart's opera *Don Giovanni* had its world premiere in 1787.
- **Josefov** – the former Jewish ghetto with the **Old-New Synagogue**, the oldest active synagogue in Europe, and the **Old Jewish Cemetery**, where the gravestones are crowded on top of each other.

### The New Town

Despite its name, the New Town was founded by Charles IV in 1348.

- **Wenceslas Square** – in fact a 750-metre-long boulevard, originally a horse market. At its top stand the statue of **St Wenceslas**, the patron saint of the Czech lands, and the **National Museum**. The square has witnessed key moments of modern Czech history: independence in 1918, Jan Palach's self-immolation in protest against the Soviet occupation in 1969, and the mass demonstrations of the Velvet Revolution in 1989.
- **The National Theatre** on the bank of the Vltava – a symbol of the National Revival. It opened in 1881, burnt down shortly afterwards and was rebuilt, again thanks to public collections, by 1883.
- **The Municipal House** – a jewel of Art Nouveau next to the Gothic **Powder Tower**.
- **The Dancing House** (1996) – a modern building whose two towers look like a dancing couple.

### Outside the centre

- **Vyšehrad** – the second castle of the Přemyslids on a rock above the Vltava. Its cemetery, with the **Slavín** tomb, is the resting place of many famous Czechs, for example the composers Bedřich Smetana and Antonín Dvořák and the writers Karel Čapek and Jan Neruda.
- **Petřín Hill** – a park with a **viewing tower** built in 1891 as a smaller copy of the Eiffel Tower, a mirror maze and an observatory. You can get there by **funicular**.

## Culture and everyday life

Prague is one of the cultural centres of Central Europe. Opera, ballet and drama can be seen at the National Theatre and the State Opera, concerts of classical music at the **Rudolfinum** and the Municipal House, and the international music festival **Prague Spring** takes place every year. Visitors also like Prague's speciality, **black light theatre**. The most important museums are the **National Museum** and the **National Gallery**.

Getting around is easy thanks to a dense network of **public transport**: three **metro** lines (A, B and C, with a fourth line, D, under construction), trams, buses, the funicular to Petřín and even ferries across the Vltava. Václav Havel Airport connects Prague with the rest of the world.

## Prague today – things to talk about

Prague is one of the most visited cities in Europe, which brings money and jobs, but it also causes problems. The historic centre is often overcrowded, many flats have been turned into short-term rentals, rents are rising and fewer and fewer locals actually live in the centre. That is why the city tries to attract tourists to other districts and to limit, for example, noisy pub crawls. When you talk about Prague in the exam, it is good to show that you can see both sides.
